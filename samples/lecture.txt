Today I'd like to look at urban beekeeping, which has become surprisingly popular in European cities over the last decade. At first, many people assumed that bees would struggle in a polluted environment, surrounded by traffic and concrete. In fact, researchers have found that city colonies often produce more honey than those in the countryside. One explanation is the remarkable variety of plants in parks, private gardens and even on balconies. Farmland, by contrast, is frequently dominated by a single crop, which only flowers for a few weeks each year. However, the rapid growth in the number of hives has created a new problem: competition for food. Wild pollinators, such as solitary bees and hoverflies, may be pushed out of areas where hives are concentrated. For this reason, some local authorities now require beekeepers to register their hives and limit how many can be kept on a single roof. Conservation groups argue that planting native wildflowers is a more effective way to support insects than simply adding more honeybees.
